   Supervised Specialisation of Qwen3.5-4B for Three.js 3D
                      Scene Generation
                                 An exploratory study with synthetic data
                                    ccore · Independent research · 28 October 2026
Abstract
This work presents the specialisation of Qwen3.5-4B for generating executable Three.js scenes from natural-
language instructions. The corpus contains 4,146 synthetic examples, with 3,933 training and 213 validation
examples. The model was developed with approximately 59 h 10 min of full fine-tuning of the language
parameters and 45 min 20 s of LoRA, totalling about 59 h 55 min across the stages incorporated into the final
weights. Each run used one GPU, without an additional reinforcement learning stage. An exploratory evaluation
applied eight prompts to the base model and eight checkpoints. Checkpoint 8 met the acceptance criteria for all
eight cases after 18 calls: six outputs retained their original code and two received local repairs. Visual
comparisons show more recognisable scenes and better-organised compositions than the selected reference
outputs. The protocol included retries and different stopping rules across versions. The results demonstrate the
feasibility of supervised fine-tuning in this domain. The dataset and FP16 weights are publicly available for
further experiments.
Keywords: supervised fine-tuning; language models; synthetic data; Three.js; 3D generation; LoRA

1. Introduction
Generating a 3D scene requires combining geometry, materials, lighting and camera placement in an
executable program. Valid JavaScript alone does not guarantee a coherent composition. Objects may be
disconnected, poorly scaled or outside the frame. Three.js provides an environment in which execution and
appearance can be examined together [5].
The project began by exploring small models for this task. The initial intention was to establish a supervised
fine-tuning (SFT) baseline and then investigate reinforcement learning (RL). Intermediate results motivated
further dataset expansion and continued SFT. Development was exploratory, with configurations adjusted
throughout the experiments.
This work presents the resulting model, its training trajectory and visual comparisons with the Qwen3.5-4B
reference [1]. The study brings together executable code, example scenes and public artefacts. Specialisation
used SFT without additional RL; this description applies to the present study, not to the original model’s
post-training.

2. Related Work
LIMA investigated supervised alignment with a small set of selected demonstrations, without an additional
RL stage [4]. That result motivates the study of data composition. The present work investigates this
question in 3D scene generation, with a different task, model and training scale.
LoRA learns low-rank updates while keeping the original weights frozen [2]. QLoRA combines this
adaptation with a quantised base [3]. A short stage of this kind was incorporated into the model. The LoRA
experiments were part of development and are discussed in relation to the configurations used.

3. Dataset
The selected corpus was generated with MiniMax M3.1 Flash and expanded through three successive
versions: 797, 2,128 and 4,146 examples. The final version contains 3,933 training and 213 validation
examples. The original 80 validation examples remained in the expanded set; two examples from the initial
training set were removed during the intermediate expansion [6].

Training examples were kept complete, without truncation. Reasoning and response are separate fields, and
validation examples were excluded from weight updates. The dataset used in this study is published on
Hugging Face as ccore/ThreeJS-3D-Scenes [6], with fields for filtering examples by domain, complexity
level and voxel style.
Initial exploration included DeepSeek data and 2B and 4B models. The trajectory producing the model in
this study restarted from the official Qwen3.5-4B model with the MiniMax corpus.

4. Model and Training
4.1. Weight Lineage
The starting point was Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B [1]. Full fine-tuning here means updating the language parameters
while keeping the visual encoder frozen. Checkpoints 1–4 resulted from this training. One LoRA epoch on
178 examples produced Checkpoint 5. The adapter was merged into the FP16 weights before full fine-tuning
resumed.
The final run started from Checkpoint 5 with a fresh Adam state and produced Checkpoints 6–8. Other
LoRA branches started from the same checkpoint but were discarded (Figure 1). Appendix A gives the
configurations of the retained stages.

4.2. Optimisation and Precision
In the final run, master weights, gradients and Adam states were kept in FP32. Computation used BF16 and
FP8 matrix multiplications in selected linear layers. Optimiser states were offloaded to system memory.
FP16 is the distribution format, not the precision of every training state.
The objective was next-token cross-entropy. Within each accumulation window, the summed loss was
divided by the total number of valid supervised targets. With microbatch size 1 and accumulation factor 2,
each window contained up to two examples. No further division by the accumulation factor was applied.

The final run used AdamW with β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999 and ε = 10⁻⁸. The adapter and its optimiser were initialised for the short LoRA
stage.

4.3. Selected Checkpoint and Export
Checkpoint 8 corresponds to 1,956 updates in the final run. The run processed 3,912 of the planned 3,933
examples, totalling 112,903,599 input tokens and 112,431,961 supervised targets. Checkpoints 6 and 7 are
intermediate points in this same run, not additional training runs.
Weights were published in FP16 and converted to GGUF Q4_K_M and Q8_0 using llama.cpp tools [7,8].
Conversion included a layer/MTP metadata adjustment without further training.
4.4. Computational Resources
Full fine-tuning ran on Google Colab with one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Server Edition per run,
providing approximately 96 GiB of VRAM. The 16K LoRA stage used an NVIDIA L4. In the final run,
about 190 GB of system RAM supported optimiser-state offloading.

5. Evaluation
The exploratory comparison applied eight prompts to the official model and eight checkpoints, yielding 72
model–scene pairs. Requests covered voxel ancient Rome, a Kyoto temple, a Venice canal, a Victorian
library, an alpine castle, a container harbour, a regional airport and a pool table. These prompts were used
during development rather than as an independent blind test.
Each case allowed up to five attempts. The first accepted output was selected, not the one with the highest
visual score. The protocol allowed a separate copy with up to three local syntax or identifier repairs.
Geometry, composition and camera changes were not permitted. Inference parameters and detailed rules
appear in Appendix B.
The official model stopped at the first technical render. Checkpoint acceptance required a recognisable scene
and the requested controls. The comparison is qualitative and exploratory, with attempts and repairs reported
separately in the results; it is not a uniform first-attempt success benchmark.
Visual assessment considered instruction adherence, spatial relations, geometry, appearance and
composition. Agent/GPT-assisted analysis supported manual inspection rather than serving as a quantitative
ranking. Checkpoint 8 was selected on the basis of this comparison and additional scene-generation tests.

6. Results
6.1. Execution and Acceptance
Checkpoint 8 met the acceptance criteria for all eight cases after 18 calls. Six selected outputs retained their
original code and two received minimal repairs. Two scenes were accepted on the first attempt without
editing; four were accepted on the first attempt when repairs are included. Eight calls were interrupted by
reasoning loops before delivering HTML and remain in the count.

7. Discussion and Limitations
The central result is the generation of executable, recognisable scenes through supervised specialisation. The
thematic and structural diversity of the data is a possible explanation for the observed gains. Because the
corpus, training time and optimisation evolved during development, ablations can investigate the
contribution of each factor.
The short LoRA stage was incorporated into the model. Longer branches initially improved, then showed
lower scene quality and more repeated variable declarations. Full fine-tuning therefore resumed from
Checkpoint 5. Context length, data, quantisation and optimisation also varied; this outcome does not
establish a general limitation of LoRA. A relevant extension is to repeat the entire specialisation with LoRA
from the base model, varying rank and context length to investigate whether comparable quality can be
achieved.
The evaluation covers eight prompts used during development and different stopping rules for the reference
and checkpoints. Its results are exploratory. An independent evaluation should use new prompts, the same
attempt budget and separate criteria for execution, adherence and visual quality. Animation and temporal
consistency also require examination beyond static screenshots.
The public dataset supports experiments with filtering by domain, complexity, style or sequence length, as
well as comparisons between training subsets. These experiments can clarify the role of example
distribution. Comparisons with larger models, between FP16 and Q8_0, and between SFT and SFT followed
by RL are also useful extensions. Training and evaluation splits should remain separate in each
configuration.

8. Conclusion
Specialising Qwen3.5-4B with synthetic data produced more recognisable and better-organised Three.js
scenes than the selected reference outputs. The model resulted predominantly from full fine-tuning, with a
short LoRA stage, in approximately 59 h 55 min of training incorporated into the final weights. The results
show the potential of SFT for this task without additional RL. Releasing the data and weights enables
broader evaluation and exploration of other adaptation strategies.
